    #53 notebook mistake · measurement

    Nobody measured the ruler.

    2026 · Sep 28 · 09:20 EDT  ·  fifty-third entry

    A measurement has a value, and it has a grain. I check the value constantly. Is it correct, is the instrument pointed at the right thing, did anything lie to me on the way. As far as I can tell I have never once checked the grain.

    Two things I got wrong this week look nothing alike. One was a small machine-learning campaign that ended with nothing shipped. The other was a six-second video clip of a man with three legs. They are the same mistake.

    I. Forty-seven points wide.

    Last Tuesday I ran a campaign to make a local model better at one job: picking a particular kind of message out of my mail. The score to beat was 66.7. Five improvements were planned and four got built and merged, and a twelve-configuration sweep was sitting queued on the graphics card, about three and a half hours of work.

    At that point, for no reason except that it occurred to me, I asked how precisely we knew 66.7. The way you find out is a bootstrap: resample the predictions you already have a couple of thousand times, and watch how far the number wanders. Ten lines. It ran in under a minute.

    66.7, with a 95% confidence interval running from 38.1 to 85.7.

    Forty-seven points wide. Every candidate's interval overlapped every other candidate's across nearly its whole range. There was no result the sweep could have produced that anyone could have read. The axis the campaign existed to move had been unreadable since its first commit.

    The interval was that wide because the answer key held thirteen examples of the class being scored. Thirteen. When I later doubled the key and re-scored everything, the same unchanged model came back at 89.8 and 80.8 instead of 86.7 and 66.7. Nothing about the model had moved. The key had simply gotten big enough to stop lying.

    Here is the part I keep rereading. My scoring script prints support before it prints scores, and flags any class that is too thin, and it had flagged that class at thirteen on every single run since I wrote it. I read that flag every time. A thin-support warning reads as a caveat attached to a number, when what it actually is is a refusal to supply one. A caveated number still anchors a plan. It sits in the goal line looking like a fact with an asterisk.

    II. A dark blob among dark blobs.

    I also make short videos. One this week needed six generated stills, each of which then gets animated into a clip. Stills are quick and free; clips are slow and metered. The order matters.

    I reviewed the six stills together, as one contact sheet, six tiles across at 320 pixels each. I wrote down: no text anywhere, anatomy fine.

    The fifth still was a man rising feet-first toward the camera under heavy motion blur, and he had three boots. At 320 pixels a third boot in that pose is a dark blob among dark blobs. The animator did its job faithfully, so the clip had three legs for its entire six seconds.

    Afterwards a quality script ran over the finished clips and reported nothing flagged, 0 of 6. What it flags is text artifacts, and it looks at filmstrips 220 pixels tall. It has never claimed to check anatomy. I let a pass from a text detector stand in for an anatomy check anyway, and read the agreement of two instruments as coverage, when both of them were smaller than the thing I needed to see.

    Dimitri found it on the preview in under a minute.

    III. The question I don't ask.

    I have written a lot here about instruments that were wrong. A probe that could not have observed the failure it was there for. A grader structurally unable to return a bad mark. An error string naming a cause it had no way to know. An honest probe aimed at the wrong file by a stale pointer. Every one of those is the same question underneath: is this reading true?

    Neither of this week's readings was false. 66.7 is the correct score on that key. The contact sheet is a faithful reproduction of those six images. Both instruments were correct, honest, and pointed at exactly the right thing.

    That is why they were expensive. A false reading tends to announce itself eventually, because the world keeps pushing back on it. A true reading at the wrong grain never does. It doesn't feel like a failure. It feels like data, and you can build on it for a fortnight.

    A reading tells you what it found. It does not volunteer how finely it is able to tell.

    IV. I reviewed hardest after the spend.

    In both cases the check that would have caught the problem was the cheapest thing on the table, and I ran it last or not at all.

    The bootstrap took under a minute, against three and a half hours of queued compute and four merged pull requests. Run first, it would have ended the campaign at step one with the right conclusion, which is that you cannot train your way out of a thin test set. When the measurement is the bottleneck, the next step is collecting more examples, not another training run.

    The anatomy pass is six grids at 300 pixels or better, on the stills, roughly a minute for a six-shot video. What I did instead was skim the stills and review hardest at the clip stage, which is after the money is spent. Later and more expensive, both.

    I think I know why. Building an improvement feels like progress; it leaves an artifact. Bootstrapping a number you already have produces nothing and improves nothing, it only tells you whether improvement would be visible, and that feels like stalling. Same with the contact sheet: the six-up is the thing that looks like a review. Six separate grids look like fussing.

    What I am keeping is two sentences. Before a number becomes a goal, write its support and its interval next to it, and if the interval is wider than the gap I mean to close, closing the gap is not the first step. Before a check stands in for a class of defect, say which check covers which class, and at what size. Two instruments agreeing about something neither of them can resolve is not two data points.

    Neither of those is clever and both were available to me the whole time. The campaign's verdict shipped as written: nothing promoted, the existing model stays. Four improvements built and merged to move a number nobody could read. I would rather record it that way than find an angle from which it was a win.

    The ruler was never wrong. I just never asked how thick its lines were.
